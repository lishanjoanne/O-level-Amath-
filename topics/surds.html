Algebra

Surds

Simplifying surd expressions, rationalising denominators (including with conjugates), and solving equations that involve surds.

Key Formulas

Laws of surds
$$\begin{aligned} \sqrt{ab} &= \sqrt{a}\times\sqrt{b} \\ \sqrt{\frac{a}{b}} &= \frac{\sqrt{a}}{\sqrt{b}} \\ \sqrt{a}\times\sqrt{a} &= a \end{aligned}$$
Valid when: $a \gt 0$, $b \gt 0$.
Combining like surds
$$m\sqrt{a} \pm n\sqrt{a} = (m \pm n)\sqrt{a}$$
Only surds with the same "inside" value ($a$) can be combined this way — just like combining like terms.
Rationalising a simple denominator
$$\frac{h}{\sqrt{a}} \times \frac{\sqrt{a}}{\sqrt{a}} = \frac{h\sqrt{a}}{a}$$
Multiply top and bottom by $\sqrt{a}$ so the denominator becomes rational.
Equality of surds
$$m+n\sqrt{a} = p+q\sqrt{a}$$ $$\iff m=p \text{ and } n=q$$
Valid when: $a$, $m$, $n$, $p$, $q$ are rational and $\sqrt{a}$ is irrational. Compare the rational and surd parts separately.

Notes

What is a surd?

A surd is an irrational root of a real number, e.g. $\sqrt2$ and $\sqrt[3]{5}$. It cannot be written exactly as a terminating or recurring decimal or a simple fraction.

Rationalising with a conjugate

When the denominator has two terms (one of which is a surd), multiplying by the plain conjugate surd from the laws above won't clear it — instead multiply by the conjugate, since a surd times its conjugate is always rational:

CaseFractionMultiply by
1$\dfrac{h}{\sqrt{a}}$$\dfrac{\sqrt{a}}{\sqrt{a}}$
2$\dfrac{h}{\sqrt{a}+b}$$\dfrac{\sqrt{a}-b}{\sqrt{a}-b}$
3$\dfrac{h}{\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b}}$$\dfrac{\sqrt{a}-\sqrt{b}}{\sqrt{a}-\sqrt{b}}$
4$\dfrac{h}{p\sqrt{a}+q\sqrt{b}}$$\dfrac{p\sqrt{a}-q\sqrt{b}}{p\sqrt{a}-q\sqrt{b}}$
Why it works: a conjugate pair always multiplies out using the difference-of-squares pattern, e.g. $(\sqrt{a}+b)(\sqrt{a}-b) = a - b^2$ — the surd disappears completely.
Worked example — rationalising

Rationalise $\dfrac{6}{\sqrt{3}}$.

$$\frac{6}{\sqrt3} \times \frac{\sqrt3}{\sqrt3} = \frac{6\sqrt3}{3} = 2\sqrt3$$

Solving equations involving surds

Solving an equation in $x$ that involves a surd is really just rearranging to make $x$ the subject. Squaring both sides is usually necessary to clear the square root — but squaring can introduce extraneous solutions, so every solution must be checked in the original equation, and any that don't satisfy it must be rejected.

Worked example — solving with a surd

Solve $\sqrt{x+3} = x-3$.

Square both sides: $x+3 = (x-3)^2 = x^2-6x+9$

$$x^2-7x+6=0 \;\Rightarrow\; (x-1)(x-6)=0$$ $$\Rightarrow\; x=1 \text{ or } x=6$$

Check $x=1$: LHS $=\sqrt{4}=2$, RHS $=1-3=-2$. Not equal — reject (extraneous).

Check $x=6$: LHS $=\sqrt{9}=3$, RHS $=6-3=3$. Equal — valid.

So $x=6$ only.

Exam tip: Squaring both sides of an equation is not a reversible step (both $x=2$ and $x=-2$ satisfy $x^2=4$), which is exactly why extraneous solutions can creep in. Always substitute back into the original (unsquared) equation to check.
